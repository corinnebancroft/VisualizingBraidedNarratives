Methods

Data-Collection as Close Reading

This project is built on the idea that interpreting a novel depends as much on the small details as the broad turns of plot. As David Herman writes, “[i]n trying to make sense of narrative, interpreters attempt to reconstruct not to just what happened—who did what to or with whom, for how long, how often and in what order—but also the surrounding context or environment embedding existents, their attributes, and the actions and events in which they are more or less centrally involved” (13-14)1. From this cognitive perspective, minute details become cues that help readers re-construct or co-construct2 the storyworld. Close reading, a traditional strategy of literary interpretation, involves the attentive consideration of these small details and their possible significances. In close reading, specific details such as a particular passage or the use of a specific tense become evidence for broader claims.

This project seeks to gather the sort of evidence one would notice while reading closely and collect it in a format that allows us to use digital tools to identify and visualize the patterns that emerge across a large body of evidence. While other digital projects have abandoned close reading practices in favour of “distant reading,” this project maintains a commitment to attending closely to the text and using those very details for a scaled-up analysis. To adopt Paul Martin Eve’s metaphor3, digital methods can function both as a microscope, exposing intricate nuances of individual texts, and as a telescope, illuminating patterns across texts. To achieve these scope-like results, we treat data entry as close-reading evidence gathering without pre-existing broader claims. We collect evidence related to formal structure (how is the novel organized into sections and sub-sections?), character networks (how do the characters in the novel form into communities?), and plot events (what happens and when do we learn about it?). After reading the novel and discussing it with the team, research assistants (RAs) begin collecting data. In the pilot project, they entered information into a relational database. If the project receives further funding, we will use Extensible Markup Language (XML) following the Text Encoding Initiative (TEI) guidelines. In some cases, the information the RAs enter into the database is an objective observation such as the title or length of a chapter. In other instances, the information entered is the result of a critical interpretation. For instance, close readers of The Sound and the Fury know that the golfer’s call “caddie” references both the person who carries their clubs and Caddy Compson. We discuss situations where the RA cannot interpret the evidence presented as a team and work to land on a reasonable interpretation.

The methods for data-collection specific to each visualization are detailed in the following sections. In all visualizations, we use the printed page as the smallest unit of analysis, which corresponds to the dominant citation practices in the discipline.

Braid Visualizations

Data-Collection: RAs enter information about the novel’s organization into the “container table” of the database. We use the term container to refer to any portion of text that is demarcated as a unit. This process involves analyzing the novel’s organization into parts, chapters, sub-sections, and embedded narratives. For instance, a novel divided into two parts will have two “level one” containers. The RAs specify the title of each container, its start page, end page, and narrator (if it has only one). Each of these two parts may contain multiple chapters, which the RAs record as “level two” containers. The RAs specify the same information about title, start page, end page, and narrator. In addition, they indicate that a chapter in Part 1 takes Part 1 as a parent.

This process is straightforward for formally marked containers such as parts, chapters, and even labelled sub-sections, but it’s also possible to have containers without formal titles such as embedded narratives. We include informal containers in the “container table” in the following situations:

  • If the new container has a different narrator than that of the parent container; for instance, embedded narratives often feature a character narrator different than that of the parent container.
  • If the new container is typographically marked as distinct; for instance, letters not only tend to have a different narrator but also are often rendered in a different font with different sized margins than the parent container.
  • If the narrator identifies the container as separate from the parent; for instance, in The History of Love, character-narrator Leo announces that he is writing a book: "I wrote a sentence:" (9); the italics that follow the colon belong to that book "Once upon a time there was a boy" (9). This is an embedded narrative identified by the narrator.

We do not include dialogue as embedded narratives. We can have several levels in this structure. For instance, a letter in an embedded narrative in a chapter of a part would be a level four container.

We used Adobe Illustrator to make the formal structure infographics. Our current process visualizes level one and level two containers. You can find the steps here: Braid Method (PDF).

Social Network Visualizations

Data-Collection: Social Network diagrams connect nodes with edges. For our purposes, each character represents a node and the relationship between characters represent an edge. Some who use social networking visualization adopt an exchange as the criterion for a relationship4, but this method, devised for drama, misses much of what happens in a novel, a form that depends on the nuances of focalization, observation, perception, and memory. Consider the classic Peanuts comic strip. Charlie Brown has a significant crush on the Little Red-Haired Girl, but he never actually talks to her. Methods that focus only on exchanges would exclude her from a character network. A similar exclusion may occur in an analysis of Harry Potter and the Sorcerer’s Stone. Aside from a few lines of dialogue at the end of the novel, Voldemort does not have any exchanges with anyone. A social network graph that relies only on exchanges would render this major villain as a more minor character than Harry’s cousin Dudley.

Our method involves documenting evidence of a relationship, the way one does while close reading, rather than limiting the kinds of relationships included. Because social network graphing visualizes relationships, we strove to be as inclusive as possible with what constitutes a relationship. We record a relationship if there is any evidence of a relationship between the characters. This evidence can include: a traditional exchange, the declaration of a relationship (the phrase “Mr and Mrs Dursley” declares a marriage between the two), a physical interaction (Voldemort tried to kill Harry), a reference to another character (McGonagall said that He Who Must Not Be Named…), and perception and thought (Charlie Brown thought about the Little Red-Haired Girl). In the pilot project, we distinguished between exchanges and relationships, even though all exchanges are evidence of a relationship, to explore the affordances of each method. We discovered that including relationships rather than just exchanges offers a more robust and complete visualization of the novel. If the project receives further funding, we will abandon the distinction between exchanges and relationships and instead distinguish between direct and indirect relationships. A direct relationship is one where two characters actually meet, and an indirect relationship is one where one character only knows about another character. We also marked the direction of a relationship. Charlie Brown’s admiration for the Little-Red-Haired Girl is a one way relationship. The Dursley’s marriage is a two-way or reciprocal relationship. Because we record the page on which a relationship occurs, the HCMC programmers are able to use the Story Space relationship data to create tables of Text Space relationships. Text Space relationships are relationships exist between characters who appear on the same page regardless of whether they have a relationship in the storyworld. For further information on our data collection method see here: Relationships Method (PDF).

We use Gephi, a free, open-source network analysis and visualization software, to make the social network visualizations. This involves exporting the relationship data from the relational database as two .csv files. The first is a node file where each character is assigned a numerical ID. The second is an edges file that documents the relationships between characters (although the file only uses their numerical ID). After importing the spreadsheets into Gephi, we use the software to perform a number of analyses and algorithms that modify the size, placement, and colour of the nodes and edges. Those steps are delineated here: Gephi Method (PDF).

Time Graphs

Data collection: After reading the novel and gathering all the information relevant for the formal structure and social network visualizations, the project team is quite familiar with the novel’s plot. Each individual develops a list of major plot events that should be included in a timeline of the novel. If an event meets one of the following criteria, we consider it a major event:

  • The event is a kernel event; Seymour Chatman defines a kernel as “narrative moments that give rise to cruxes in the direction taken by events. They are nodes of hinges in the structure, branching points which force a movement into one or two (or more) possible paths” (53)5.
  • The event has major thematic value; the event may not move the plot forward, but does relate to the novel’s major thematic concerns.

After each team member has developed their list, we compare each against the others. This process reveals consensus about many key events. We use the criteria and evidence from the novel to resolve any conflicts. After agreeing on a list of events, we determine, to the best of our ability, the story time when the event occurred. In many instances, we can identify a fictional date, which allows us to graph absolute time. In cases where we cannot, the relational database allows us to indicate that a given event follows another event, which allows us to graph the relative time. After developing the list of events and their story times, the RAs go back through the novel and document all mentions of each event. This will document their text times. The HCMC programmers use D3, a free, open-source JavaScript library, to plot story time against text time. RAs also indicate which characters participate in each event. The HCMC programmers manipulate the colour of the point representing the event to indicate which major characters participate. We calculated which characters are represented through the weighted degree function in Gephi.
